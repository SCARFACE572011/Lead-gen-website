Ask ten web designers how they get clients and eight will say "referrals", which is another way of saying they don't control their pipeline. Referrals are great until they dry up, and paid ads eat margin before you've proven an offer. The designers who stay booked without either share one habit: **they prospect businesses that visibly need a website, every week, on a system.**

This is that system.

## The best web design client already exists. They just have no site

Every city has hundreds of profitable businesses running on nothing but a Google listing and word of mouth: contractors, salons, auto shops, restaurants, cleaners. They have revenue and customers. What they don't have is a website, which means:

- **No incumbent to displace.** You're not fighting "we already have a web guy."
- **A pitch you can make in one sentence.** "Customers searching for you can't find you."
- **A fast, provable win.** A one-page site with services, photos, and a click-to-call button changes their business visibly in week one.

Compare that with pitching a redesign to a business that already has a site: longer sales cycle, subjective taste battles, and a cheaper competitor around every corner. No-website businesses are simply the better market. The problem has only ever been *finding them at scale*.

## Step 2: Verify the gap before you pitch

Nothing kills credibility like "you don't have a website" sent to a business that has one. Two-minute check per lead:

1. Google their exact business name + city. No site in the top results?
2. Check their Google Business Profile for a website link. A Facebook page or a Linktree doesn't count. That's your opening, not your obstacle.
3. Glance at their reviews. Solid rating + no site = thriving business that's leaving money on the table. That's your best prospect.

## Step 3: Pitch with proof, not a portfolio

Web design cold outreach fails when it opens with the designer ("I build beautiful, responsive websites…"). It works when it opens with *their* business. The highest-converting move costs you 30 minutes: **mock up their homepage before they ask.**

> **Subject:** made something for [Business Name]
>
> Hi [first name],
>
> I noticed [Business Name] doesn't have a website. When people search "[category] in [city]," your competitors show up and you don't.
>
> I design sites for [city] businesses, so I went ahead and sketched what your homepage could look like: [screenshot or link]
>
> If you like it, I can have the real thing live in about a week. If not, no worries, the mockup's yours either way.
>
> [Your name]

Send that to ten verified no-website businesses and you will get conversations. The mockup does what no portfolio can: it makes the decision concrete. (More openers and follow-ups: [cold email templates for local outreach](/blog/cold-email-templates-local-business-outreach).)

**No email on file? Call.** No-website businesses skew owner-operated, so the person answering the phone is often the decision-maker. "I build websites for [category] businesses in [city], and yours came up as one of the best-reviewed without a site. Ninety seconds. Can I tell you what that's costing you?"

## Step 4: Price for the follow-on, not the site

The first site is the beginning of the account, not the end:

- **Build:** $1,500–$5,000 for a small-business site (one to five pages, mobile-first, click-to-call, reviews embedded).
- **Care plan:** $50–$150/month for hosting, edits, and updates. Twenty care plans is a salary floor before you sell anything else.
- **The upsell path:** the business that just got its first website is the same business that needs Google Business optimization, review automation, and local SEO next quarter. ## Step 5: Make it a weekly loop

The designers who never worry about their pipeline run the same loop every week, and it takes about two hours:

1. **Monday:** pull a fresh scored list, new ZIP or new category. ([Territory strategy here](/blog/prospect-local-clients-by-zip-code).)
2. **Tuesday–Thursday:** verify the top 15, send 10–15 pitches (one mockup for the best prospect).
3. **Friday:** follow up on last week's batch. Half your replies come from the follow-up, not the opener.
4. Track three numbers: sent, replied, booked. Double down where replies come from.

Do that for six weeks and the "where's my next client coming from" anxiety disappears. You'll know your numbers well enough to predict it.
