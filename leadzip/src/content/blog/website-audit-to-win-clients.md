A local business owner hears the same pitch every week. "We'll get you more customers" arrives by email, by phone, and occasionally in person while they're halfway through a customer's haircut. They've learned to tune it out, and honestly, they should. Every pitch is a claim, and claims from strangers are worth nothing.

An audit is a different move. Instead of telling an owner what you could do, you show them what you found. "Your Google profile is unclaimed and your site doesn't load on a phone" isn't marketing. It's information about their own business that they didn't have, delivered by the one person who bothered to look. That's why audits open doors that pitches can't: they replace a promise with proof of work already done.

This guide covers what to check, how to present the findings to an owner without burying them in jargon, and a free tool that does the first pass in seconds.

## Why audits work when pitches don't

Four reasons, all of them about trust:

- **Proof beats promises.** You did unpaid work before asking for anything. Owners notice, because almost nobody else does that.
- **It's about their business, not yours.** A pitch opens with your agency. An audit opens with their review count. Guess which one an owner keeps reading.
- **The ask is small.** "Can I show you three things I found?" is a five-minute yes. "Can we talk about a monthly retainer?" is a no from a stranger.
- **It frames you as a diagnostician.** Owners live this logic in their own trade. A good HVAC tech inspects the unit before quoting. An agency that audits before pitching reads as the same kind of professional.

## What to check: four areas, no paid tools required

A useful client audit doesn't need enterprise software. It needs the discipline to check the same things every time.

### 1. Site presence and basics

Start with the blunt questions. Does a website exist at all? Does it load over HTTPS without a browser warning? Does it render on a phone, where most local searches happen, without sideways scrolling or unreadable text? Is it a real domain, or a Facebook page doing a website's job? Then the two-tap test: can a visitor find services and hours within two taps, and is the phone number tappable? A site that fails the two-tap test loses callers it will never know about.

### 2. Speed, the way a customer feels it

Skip the lab reports at this stage. Open the site on your phone, on mobile data, and count. If you're waiting long enough to notice you're waiting, so is every customer, and oversized images are the usual culprit on small-business sites. "Your site took about six seconds to load on my phone" is more persuasive to an owner than any performance score, because it's the exact experience their customers are having.

### 3. Reviews

Reviews are the storefront most owners forget they have. Check the count against two or three direct competitors nearby, the average rating, the date of the most recent review, and whether the owner replies. A 4.9 rating across nine reviews sitting next to a competitor's 4.6 across three hundred is a visibility gap, not a quality gap. That distinction is a finding an owner will actually thank you for.

### 4. Listings and the Google Business Profile

The profile is often the first thing a customer sees and the last thing an owner touches. Is it claimed? Are the hours current and the category right? Are the photos owner-uploaded or just Street View? Do the name, address, and phone number match across the profile, the website, and Yelp? Mismatched hours alone send real customers to locked doors, which makes this the easiest finding to explain of them all.

## The 10-point client audit template

Run the same checklist on every prospect and audits stop being a project and start being a habit. Copy this one:

```
CLIENT AUDIT - [Business name], [date]

 1. Website exists and loads over https        PASS / FAIL
 2. Readable on a phone, no sideways scroll    PASS / FAIL
 3. Loads fast on mobile data                  PASS / FAIL
 4. Phone number is tap-to-call                PASS / FAIL
 5. Services and hours findable in two taps    PASS / FAIL
 6. Google Business Profile claimed            PASS / FAIL
 7. Hours, address, phone match everywhere     PASS / FAIL
 8. Review count near top local competitors    PASS / FAIL
 9. A new review in the last few months        PASS / FAIL
10. Owner replies to reviews                   PASS / FAIL

Top three findings, in customer terms:
1.
2.
3.
```

Ten checks, one page, maybe fifteen minutes once you've done a few. The last block matters most, and it's the subject of the next section.

## How to present findings to an owner

Most audits die in the delivery. An owner who feels lectured stops listening, and an owner handed a ten-page PDF files it in the trash. Five rules keep the conversation alive:

- **Open with something genuinely good.** "Your rating is excellent, better than most shops I looked at" earns you the right to deliver the rest. It also happens to be honest, which owners can smell.
- **Cap it at three findings.** Ten findings is a shopping list from a stranger. Three findings is a diagnosis. Pick the ones with the clearest line to lost customers.
- **Translate every finding into customers, not metrics.** Not "your NAP data is inconsistent" but "Google says you're open until 6, your website says 5, so some customers are showing up to a locked door."
- **Give the finding away, sell the fix.** Tell them exactly what's wrong, free, no strings. The knowledge costs you nothing and the labor is what they'll pay for.
- **Keep it to one page with their name on it.** A shareable link is even better, because owners forward links to business partners and spouses. Paper gets left on the counter.

Delivered this way, an audit doesn't feel like a pitch at all. It feels like a favor, which is exactly the door you want to walk through.
